# Where the work stands (2026-09-15, end of session)

Written because the session ended mid-change and the reasoning behind every
number here took measurement to get. Read this before touching the picture path.

## The goal, as the viewer stated it

The published version is neither sharp enough nor smooth enough. Rebuild it
borrowing from `pocket-tv`, and **maximise both at once, measuring to the
device's limit**. Pictures must follow each source's own aspect ratio. Both a
higher resolution and a higher frame rate are wanted, and one is not to be
traded for the other without saying so.

## What the link can carry, measured

The single most useful number in this session, and the one that took longest to
establish honestly:

| Quantity | Value | How |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Total throughput | **~175 kB/s** | Device-reported `in_bps`, stable across every configuration tried |
| Picture's share | **~145 kB/s** | Derived: total minus the sound's fixed cost |
| Sound's cost | **32 kB/s** | Protocol fixes it at 50 packets × 640 B a second |

A sweep from 6 to 14 frames a second pinned the picture at 143–148 kB/s
regardless of the rate asked for, and showed the sound losing 24% of its
packets the moment the picture took more. That is the ceiling, and it is shared.

> This was the first measurement, at the geometry of the time. It was taken
> again after the move to 200x150 and the conclusion held while the curve got
> clearer — see "The link's ceiling, confirmed" below. The 156 kB/s there is
> what 12 frames a second actually runs at, below this 175: **a rate that runs
> clean should not be sitting on the ceiling.**

## What a picture costs

Measured on one live clip, 16:9 content, at eight frames a second. The byte
cost is very nearly proportional to the pixels, and slightly worse than
proportional as the picture shrinks:

| Box | Pixels | Bytes/frame | Bytes/pixel | Frames a second |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 320x240 | 76800 | 22455 | 0.292 | 6.5 |
| 288x216 | 62208 | 19407 | 0.312 | 7.5 |
| 256x192 | 49152 | 15954 | 0.325 | 9.1 |
| 224x168 | 37632 | 12959 | 0.344 | 11.2 |
| 192x144 | 27648 | 10044 | 0.363 | 14.4 |
| 160x120 | 19200 | 7506 | 0.391 | 19.3 |

The last row is the published version, and the third column of that row is why
it runs at twelve frames a second: it sends a quarter of the pixels this one
does.

## All three settings compared -- 280x210 is where it lands

**The old rejection of 320x240 had expired, and re-testing it did not change the
choice.** That rejection was measured while the audio lead was still 200 ms, and
the lead was not settled until **an hour and twenty minutes later** -- the premise
had expired, so it was measured again.

| Setting | Content pixels | fps | Picture a second | Link | Underruns | Draw/frame | Own pixels |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 240x180 | 32,400 | 13.9 | 450k | 192 kB/s | 0 | 63.5 ms | 3/4 |
| **280x210 (default)** | **43,960** | **10.1** | **444k** | **203 kB/s** | **0** | **48.9 ms** | **7/8** |
| 320x240 | 57,600 | 8.9 | 547k | 219 kB/s | **4** | 34.7 ms | all |

Three things follow:

- **240x180 and 280x210 deliver almost exactly the same picture a second -- 450k
  against 444k pixels -- and 280x210 gets there with less enlargement**, so it is
  the better of the two. That is what the "pixels a second" measure is for: once
  two settings are level on it, what remains to compare is the picture itself.
- **320x240 delivers the most pixels a second (547k) and draws a frame fastest
  (34.7 ms, because there is no enlargement step), and cannot hold a session**: at
  219 kB/s it sits against the top of the link's measured 141 to 243, underrunning
  four times in 300 seconds and losing eleven connections to the device.
- **Less enlargement draws faster** (34.7, 48.9, 63.5 ms as the ratio goes 1/1,
  8/7, 4/3). The cost is not computation but bandwidth: not enlarging means sending
  every pixel at full resolution.

**A note on expired premises**: when a proposal is rejected, the premise it was
rejected under has to be recorded with it. Bigger audio chunks and the 320x240
rejection are both in this class -- when the premise changes the old conclusion
has to be re-measured, not quoted.

## The picture moves up a step: 280x210, enlarged 8/7, both settings switchable

The share of the panel carrying a pixel of its own goes from **three quarters**
(enlarged 4/3) to **seven eighths** (8/7). Content pixels rise from 32,400 to
**43,960 (+36%)**. Both settings were measured:

| Setting | Content pixels | Median fps | Draw per frame | Sound | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 240x180 | 32,400 | 13.9 | 63.5 ms | full | 192 kB/s |
| **280x210 (current default)** | **43,960** | **10.1** | **54.7 ms** | full | 203 kB/s |

10.1 frames is far above the four the viewer once described as "like a
slideshow", and a 36% increase in pixels is visible, so 280x210 it is.

**Switching**: `TV_GEOMETRY=240x180` or `TV_GEOMETRY=280x210`. Both are in the
build, but **the device compiles its geometry in and the two ends must match
exactly** -- a mismatch refuses every session, and a refusal looks like a broken
link rather than a configuration error. So the geometry is chosen **by one
name** rather than by three variables that must be set together; a name the
firmware does not have fails at start-up with the options listed, and
`tools/check_config_agreement.py` prints which pane the firmware was built for
against which the server is about to send.

**The rate ceiling is tied to the geometry** (`TV_MAX_FPS`, twelve at 280x210).
The ceiling tracks the link as much as the panel: at 240x180 a frame is 12.1 kB
and fourteen frames is 192 kB/s, which the link carried; at 280x210 a frame is
16.0 kB and fourteen is 256 kB/s against a link measured between 141 and 243.
**A ceiling that suits one geometry is an overload for the other.**

## The frame-rate ceiling moved from 11 to 14 -- median rate 10.5 to 13.4

**Eleven was the wrong number.** It was set on the reasoning that a frame's
74 ms `decode_max_ms` peak needs a slot wider than itself -- but that peak is one
frame in a hundred, and **a sustained rate runs on the mean** (63.5 ms).

Measured at a fixed fifteen frames a second for 200 seconds: the device held
**14.1 to 15.0 frames** with the sound full and no underruns. The only cost was a
burst of 53 dropped frames at the start, which then stopped -- **the picture
aligning itself with the audio clock once**, not a device that cannot keep up.

| Config | Median fps | Range | Dropped | Sound |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Adaptive, ceiling 11 (old) | 10.5 | 9.2-11.1 | 38 | full |
| Fixed 15 | 14.4 | 13.8-15.0 | 631 | full |
| **Adaptive, ceiling 14 (now)** | **13.4** | 8.8-14.1 | **11** | full |

### The distinction the controller was missing

It stepped back for any dropped frame at all, so at the edge of thirteen to
fourteen it oscillated: over 300 seconds the median was 11.9 while a fixed
fourteen held 14.4 -- **the session was spent climbing rather than at the top**.

A rate now drops only when **two consecutive windows both lose frames**. An
isolated drop is recorded and nothing more, and it no longer clears the run of
comfortable windows the climb depends on. The windows needed to raise the rate
went from four to two.

### Rejected: deepening the pipeline (a fourth packet buffer)

The reason to try was `nobuf` -- the receiver running out of free buffers --
going non-zero at thirteen to fourteen frames, which the controller read as a
device that cannot keep up.

But the fourth buffer wanted 16 KB, and **the channel list is parsed after the
session's buffers are allocated**, so the two compete for one heap. cJSON then
failed to allocate and returned NULL, every CONFIG field looked missing to the
device, and every session dropped on connect.

**What that looks like from outside is not "out of memory" but "configuration
rejected"**, indistinguishable from a link fault or an unstable network. This is
the third time this trap has been sprung: **anything added to a session is spent
before the channel list is parsed.**

To save the next person the time, `config_valid` now prints the server's value and
the device's expectation **field by field** on rejection, and the heap and largest
free block when the parse itself fails. It used to report only that something had
been rejected, which is exactly what let this hide three times.

## An instrument that had been read wrong: the `panel_ms` unit error

Found while looking for a second piece of evidence for the conclusion below.
`elapsed` was **already in milliseconds** -- the divisor had been applied when the
timestamp was taken -- and it was then accumulated into a field named `panel_us`
and divided by a thousand again on the way out.

Measured, that reported 6 ms of drawing for 107 frames, or 0.06 ms a frame, while
`inflate_ms` on the same line reported 12.3 ms a frame for work that happens
inside that very interval. **Two numbers in one line that cannot both be true is
what a unit error looks like.**

Both were corrected:

| Field | Was | Now |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `panel_ms` | milliseconds accumulated as though microseconds, divided by 1000 again | microseconds accumulated, divided by 1000 on output |
| `decode_max_ms` | **session-wide maximum** (reset only at session start, so it could only climb) | per-interval peak (`atomic_exchange`, cleared on output) |
| `panel_frames` | did not exist | added, so the mean has a denominator and the instrument can be checked |

Corrected, a frame costs **63.5 ms** to draw against a 74 ms peak, and the two now
agree; `panel_frames` matches `interval_frames`, so the instrument can be
verified rather than trusted.

### The conclusion holds, but on better evidence

The previous round put the ceiling at eleven frames a second on the strength of a
field with a unit error. **Re-measured after the fix the numbers agree, and the
evidence is now sound**: 63.5 ms a frame to draw, a 74 ms peak, against a 90.9 ms
slot at eleven -- about 16 ms of slack, where twelve leaves 9. **The ceiling stays
at eleven.**

### Measured and rejected: double-buffering the stripe

The reasoning held: `push_stripe` waits for the SPI transfer at the end of every
submit, fifteen times a frame, about 31 ms -- half the frame's cost -- with the
processor idle beside the DMA engine. Two buffers would feed the panel while the
next stripe is built.

**The device showed no improvement**: 10.5 frames a second against 10.4,
`decode_max_ms` 70 against 72 to 81, panel time per frame 61 ms against a range
that was already 38 to 82. **The SPI engine is already overlapped with the next
stripe's preparation**; the wait was not the serial point it appeared to be.

The second buffer also wanted 10 KB of a heap that had none, which surfaced as the
channel list failing to parse at session start (`No memory to parse the channel
list`), and cutting the packet buffers to pay for it then cost 31 dropped frames.
Reverted in full.

## The frame-rate ceiling is the device's drawing time, not the link

This is the answer to "measure to the device's limit", and it overturns every
earlier account of where the ceiling was.

**Half the link is idle.** At eleven frames a second the picture uses about 124
kB/s against a link measured between 141 and 243. The limit is on the device: a
frame takes up to 80 to 84 ms to draw (`decode_max_ms`, steady across hundreds of
frames rather than an occasional spike).

| Rate | Slot | Slack against 80 ms | Measured |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 12 fps | 83.3 ms | 3.3 ms | the first hiccup drops a frame |
| **11 fps** | **90.9 ms** | **10.9 ms** | stable |
| 10 fps | 100 ms | 20 ms | stable |

**`MAX_FPS = 11` is derived, not probed.** It had been set by fixed-rate probes,
twelve and then fifteen, and that probe contradicted itself: asking for eighteen
frames a second failed to establish a session while twenty-one established one
and delivered 13.9. **A limit that refuses a smaller request and grants a larger
one is not a limit, it is a measurement artefact.**

Removing the ceiling and letting the controller hunt upward showed why a probe
cannot answer this: every session climbed to 14.9 frames, the receiver ran out of
free buffers (`nobuf` 0, 12, 45 within three windows), and the picture then lost
frames permanently -- **while both of the controller's signals read healthy**.
From the sending side, a fast link and a device that cannot draw look identical.

### The missing signal: dropped frames

The controller had two signals, both measured at this end of the socket -- write
duration and window bytes -- and neither can see whether the far end is keeping
up. A frame abandoned because it could not be placed in time now lowers the rate
immediately.

**Every drop counts.** Tolerating isolated drops, on the theory that one loss is
noise, was tried for 420 seconds: the median rate rose to 12.1 and the frames
lost went from 0 to 339. **When the operating point is the device's limit, a lost
frame is a signal rather than a statistic.**

Over 420 seconds in the final configuration: **no underruns, no dropped frames,
the sound full, a median of 10.4 frames a second**, with the controller moving
between ten and eleven on its own.

### Measured and rejected: merging the expansion with the enlargement

The reasoning held up -- enlarging is a copy and expanding is a lookup, the two
commute, and merging them writes 153600 fewer bytes a frame. The merged version
was verified pixel for pixel against the two-pass result and matched exactly.

**It is slower.** `decode_max_ms` went from 78 to 91, and replacing the inner
division with a multiply changed nothing (90). The cost is the lookups: two passes
do 43200 palette reads and then move bytes, one pass does 76800, one per *panel*
pixel, and a table read on this chip costs more than the byte moves it saves.
Reverted.

## The sound was being drained by the picture -- fixed, and this was the flicker

What the viewer reported as the picture coming and going was neither the link nor
the bandwidth. One task reads both streams, so while a picture packet is in
flight nothing drains the audio queue -- and a 240x180 frame is about 13 KB,
taking some 220 ms to cross, longer than the 200 ms the queue held. **Every
picture packet drained it.**

The shape of the evidence is what identified it: the ten-second interval before
each underrun had the sound *full*, 499 to 502 against a target of 500, and the
gap recorded at failure was **305 ms every single time**. An average that is
exactly right with a failure point that never moves is the signature of a timing
fault rather than a bandwidth one.

Three changes, each chosen by measurement rather than argument:

| Change | From | To | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Audio chunk | 20 ms | 40 ms | the same queue slots then hold twice the time, and it saves packets |
| Audio lead | 200 ms | 320 ms | see the sweep below |
| Video lead | tied to the audio lead | held at 200 ms | raising both released the picture 200 ms early, bunching it against the sound |

The lead sweep, 240 seconds each:

| Lead | Underruns | Median fps | Dropped |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 200 ms | 10 | — | — |
| 260 ms | 0 | 10.0 | 0 |
| **320 ms** | **0** | **10.8** | **0** |
| 400 ms | 0 | 9.0 | 39 |

**Past a point the deeper lead becomes the picture's problem**: a chunk released
long before it is due occupies the socket the frame needed. The device's
`PCM_QUEUE` went from 30 slots to 24.

### Compression level: a number in a comment has to be re-measured

`server/frames.py` said level 1 because the gap to level 6 measured under a fifth
of a percent. That was at the old geometry. At 240x180 indexed:

| Level | Bytes/frame | Relative | Compression time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 13412 | 1.000 | 0.4 ms |
| 3 | 12905 | 0.962 | 0.5 ms |
| **6** | **12147** | **0.906** | **0.9 ms** |
| 9 | 12141 | 0.905 | 1.0 ms |

**9.4% of the bytes is 9.4% of the frame rate**, and it costs half a millisecond a
frame. Over 420 seconds: median rate **9.1 to 11.9**, video traffic 143 to 121
kB/s, **dropped frames 46 to 0**.

### Memory: an exhaustion that reads as a network fault

Doubling the audio chunk while also raising `PCM_QUEUE` to 48 made every session
end with `RX connect failed errno=119` and `heap=29268 min_heap=3932`. **That
reads as a network fault and is a heap that ran out before the socket could be
opened**: doubling the chunk made 48 slots cost 60 KB, which with the 48 KB of
video buffers exceeded what was free. The slot count has to move opposite to the
chunk size, not with it.

## The bottleneck is packets a second, not bytes -- fixed

This overturns what this document said before. Pushing the frame rate up delivered
less, not more:

| Asked for | Actual fps | Video kB/s | Sound packets/10s | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 12 | 11.9 | 114 | 500, full | healthy |
| 16 | 5.6 | **83** | **262** | worse |
| 22 | 6.7 | 77 | 258 | worse |

**Ask for more and get less.** The cause is not the link but the packets: the
device's receive path tops out near 72 a second, and a 240x180 frame measures
about 13400 bytes, so anything over the then-current `AV_VIDEO_MAX` of 12288 split
into two -- twenty-four video packets a second at twelve frames, plus fifty for
the sound, is 74, straight into the ceiling. The surplus packets crowded out the
sound, whose underrun took the session down.

**The fix is to make a frame fit in one packet.** `AV_VIDEO_MAX` 12288 to 16384,
buffers four to three. Memory is unchanged to the byte: three of sixteen kilobytes
is 49152, exactly what four of twelve was, and one 16384-byte block still fits
inside the device's largest free block of 36864. After the change a request for
sixteen frames reaches 13.7 with the sound at 502.

### Three encoding-side ideas, measured and rejected

| Tried | Result |
| --- | --- |
| PNG filters sub/up/avg/paeth | all worse; the best, paeth, costs 19% more |
| Sharpening, unsharp 0.8-2.0 | unusable; error worse by 61% to 165% |
| Dropping to 64 colours | saves 30% of the bytes, adds 66% error |

The filters lose for a reason worth keeping: the indices are already quantised and
deflate's back-references exploit their repetition directly, so subtracting a
neighbour scatters the pattern instead of exposing it. Sharpening fails because
the 3-3-2 grid gives blue four levels, and ringing and banding arrive before any
perceived gain. The colour result generalises: **at a fixed budget, bytes are
better spent on pixels than on colours.**

### Adapting beats a fixed rate, measured over 300 seconds each

| Mode | Full intervals | Median fps | Lowest sound | Underruns | Dropped |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Adaptive** | **24/27** | 10.0 | **499** | **2** | **9** |
| Fixed at 12 | 13/22 | 11.1 | 472 | 8 | 33 |

The fixed rate has a median one frame higher and pays four times the underruns and
three and a half times the dropped frames for it. **Adaptive stays.**

## Settled on: 240x180 in, enlarged 4/3 on the device

**This is where the whole geometry family lands.** Every member of it -- each
multiple of twenty columns, which is each size whose stripes divide the panel's
240 rows -- was first rendered offline through the device's own enlargement and
compressed the way the server compresses, and the ones worth having were then
run on the device:

| Ratio | Arrives | Frame rate measured | Picture a second | Source pixels on screen |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | 160x120 | 12, clean | 269k | 38% |
| 8/5 | 200x150 | 12, clean | 316k | 62% |
| **4/3** | **240x180** | **11, clean** | **356k** | **75%** |
| 1/1 | 320x240 | 7, sound breaking | 403k | 100% |

**The first surprise is that enlarging does not pay.** Compressed bytes per
pixel get *worse* as the picture shrinks -- 0.28 at 320x240 against 0.33 at
160x120, measured. A small picture carries more blocking per unit of detail, so
some of what sending fewer columns saves is handed straight back. **Picture per
second falls with every step down**, which is the opposite of what this document
said before.

**The second is that 320x240 wins that column and cannot hold a session.** At
seven frames a second it fills the link and the sound underruns every forty
seconds, measured. 4/3 holds eleven frames a second with the sound full and no
dropped frames across 200 seconds -- more picture a second than any smaller
ratio can reach.

With the adaptive controller on it does better still: 200 seconds with no
interruption, no dropped frames and the sound at 499-503 packets against a
target of 500, at a median of eleven frames a second. It steps back on its own
at exactly the moments a fixed rate loses the sound, so **the shipped default is
`TV_ADAPTIVE=1` starting at ten**.

### Superseded: 200x150 in, enlarged 8/5

Sending fewer pixels and enlarging them on the panel trades sharpness for
frames, and the ratio is the knob. The ratios that keep the enlargement exact
are the ones worth using, because an exact ratio means every output pixel is a
copy of an input one, with no interpolation and no rounding:

| Ratio | Arrives | Fills | Pixels at 16:9 | Frames a second |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | 160x120 | 320x240 | 160x90 | 19.3 |
| **8/5** | **200x150** | **320x240** | **200x112** | **13.5** |
| 4/3 | 240x180 | 320x240 | 240x135 | 10.0 |

**8/5 is the one in use.** It is the only ratio that beats the published
version on both counts at once: 200x112 against 160x90 is **1.56× the pixels**,
and 13.5 against 12 is **1.13× the rate**.

The stripe geometry follows from the ratio rather than being chosen. The panel
takes sixteen rows at a time, so the picture supplies ten (16 × 5/8), and 150
rows is fifteen of those. Ten rows of 200 pixels are 2000 index bytes, which
expand to 4000 and enlarge into the panel's 10240 — the same buffer as before.
**This change costs no memory.**

### State: measured on the device

Build passes, `./tools/validate.sh --static` is green, the application was
written to `0x10000`, and **it has been measured on real hardware**. The result
is better than arithmetic predicted, and the same sweep answered the ceiling
question as a side effect:

| Asked for | Actually completed | Sound | Picture | Dropped |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **12 fps** | **117/120 = 97%** | **500/500** | 124 kB/s | **0** |
| 16 fps | 145/160 = 90% | 502/500 | 139 kB/s | climbing 14→55 |
| 20 fps | sessions will not establish | — | — | — |

**Twelve is the settled rate**, not the 13.5 that arithmetic suggested. It runs
with both streams full, no dropped frames, and 55 seconds without an
interruption, filling the whole panel.

### The link's ceiling, confirmed

That table is itself a saturation curve:

| fps | Picture | + sound 32 | Total | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 12 | 124 | 32 | **156 kB/s** | stable |
| 16 | 139 | 32 | **171 kB/s** | dropping, and the drop count climbs |
| 20 | 158 | 32 | **190 kB/s** | sessions will not establish |

**So 175 kB/s is a real ceiling, not a defect.** What establishes it is the
table above: ask for 190 kB/s and sessions stop establishing, while 156 kB/s
runs indefinitely.

I once explained this with the read-loop breakdown, and that explanation was
wrong. See the next section.

### The device has been reporting its read loop all along

`io_ms`, `wait_ms`, `iters` and `per_iter_us` appear on every line of the device
log, and every tool's regular expression stopped matching at `rx_audio`. At
twelve frames a second:

```
Per read                622 µs
Reads a second           63
io (payload drain)      0.39 s / 10 s   = 4% of thread time
wait (on queues)        0.00 s / 10 s   = 0%
Unaccounted             9.61 s / 10 s
```

**Read this breakdown by what it measures, not by what it is called.**
`io_ms` is accumulated in exactly three places -- the audio payload, the video
payload, and draining a dropped packet's payload (`av_player.c:942/984/1022`) --
and **the header read is not timed at all** (`av_player.c:887`). That is where
the receiver spends most of its wall clock: waiting for the next packet's first
byte. That time lands in neither `io` nor `wait`. So:

- **The zero for `wait` is sound, and it is this measurement's one independent
  contribution**: it shows the device is not throttling itself on its own audio
  queue, which rules out the device's own flow control as the bottleneck.
- **"The device is not the bottleneck" holds, but not because of this
  breakdown** -- it rests on the rate sweep itself (12 fps stable, 16 fps
  starting to drop, 20 fps unable to establish). Those three are independent
  evidence.
- The 96% reads accurately as "blocked in the header read, waiting for the next
  packet", not as "idle with nothing to do".

Separately, `rx_overhead_us` (`av_player.c:227`) is declared and reset but never
accumulated -- a dead field. Timing the header read too would be one `io_start`
away, but that needs another flash and the conclusion is already established
independently by the sweep, so it was not done.

## Falsified, and not to be tried again

Each of these was argued for, measured, and lost. They are recorded so the
argument is not had a second time.

- **Inter-frame differencing** — sending only the stripes that changed. Measured
  saving: **0.4%**. The stripes that repeat are the cheap ones.
- **Removing the letterbox bars** — they cost **2.93%** of a frame (658 bytes
  against 22455). Flat colour compresses to nothing.
- **Smaller packets (6 kB)** — the picture collapsed to 13.5 frames per ten
  seconds against 80 wanted. Packet *count* is not the binding constraint;
  bytes are.
- **Bigger audio chunks (40 ms)** — saves 25 packets a second and not one byte.
  No effect on the ceiling.
- **A larger WiFi receive pool** (6+16 → 6+32 buffers) — no effect at all.
- **WebRTC's congestion control** — it detects congestion through queueing
  delay. This link drops rather than queues, and the device sends no per-packet
  feedback to build a delay estimate from.

## The USB transport, built and switched off

`pocket-tv` reaches **20 frames a second at 320x180 with no dropped frames** over
the USB cable on this same panel — measured, by flashing its firmware and
running its host program. That is three times what the network path manages at
a similar pixel count.

It is implemented here behind `CONFIG_AV_USB_TRANSPORT`, default **off**, and it
should stay off. A build that prefers the cable whenever one is plugged in is a
different device, not a faster one, and that is not what this project is.

## Faults introduced during this session

Recorded because each cost real time, and two of them changed what the hardware
did rather than what the code said.

- **The USB path was wired as a preference** — the cable took over automatically
  whenever a host was attached. That changes the product's behaviour on the
  strength of a decision nobody made. Now behind an off-by-default switch.
- **The WiFi credentials were wiped.** Flashing `pocket-tv` from address `0x0`
  overwrote the `nvs` partition at `0x9000` with that image's own empty one,
  taking the stored network and server address with it. The device booted to its
  setup page. Restored from the full-chip backup taken earlier the same evening.
  **Only ever write `0x10000` on this device.** The repository says so; the
  instruction was read and then not followed.
- **Opening the serial port resets the device.** The board's auto-reset circuit
  is wired to DTR and RTS, which a fresh open toggles. Three measurement tools
  returned "no data" for this reason and it was misread as a dead source each
  time. Any tool that reads the device must open the port once and hold it.
- **`stripe_rows` was validated against the wrong constant.** `AV_STRIPE_ROWS`
  has sixteen uses on the device; fifteen of them mean "the panel takes sixteen
  rows at a time" and exactly one means "how tall a stripe the server cuts". I
  changed the picture to ten-row stripes and left that one check comparing
  against 16 — **the server sent 10, the device demanded 16, CONFIG was illegal,
  and every session connected and dropped within a second having sent no media.**
  The symptom is identical to the `channels` key collision already recorded
  here, and I should have recognised it.
  **Countermeasure:** `tools/check_config_agreement.py` compares the CONFIG the
  server will actually send, field by field, against the constants the firmware
  will actually accept, with no device involved. It is wired into
  `validate.sh --static`, so every future validation runs it. This check should
  have existed tonight: the error it catches in two seconds took me two hours
  and four reconnect cycles to find.
